## Introduction

The overarching goal of this project is to define the translational landscape of ribosomal stress and identify molecular and pathway-level features that determine how RpS12 and Xrp1 mediate translation changes under ribosomal stress. Within the scope of this course, I will address three computational questions that form the foundation of this larger investigation.
**Question 1:** How can we build a robust preprocessing pipeline that transforms raw sequencing data into analyzable translational profiles?
**Question 2:** Which transcripts show differential translation and/or transcription between ribosomal protein mutant genotypes and our control?
**Question 3:** Do ribosomal protein mutations cause altered ribosomal pausing at specific positions within transcripts?

## Dataset

As previously stated in the proposal, there are two datasets that we will be working with – bulk RNA seq and Ribo seq of the Drosophila imaginal wing disc. Bulk RNA seq measures the average expression level of individual genes across hundreds to millions of input cells and is useful to get a global idea of gene expression differences between samples. Whereas Ribo seq provides a quantitative, genome-wide "snapshot" of active protein translation by sequencing ribosome-protected mRNA fragments. It identifies precise codon-level translation, start sites, and protein-coding regions, revealing how cells regulate protein production in real-time. Bulk RNA seq and Ribo seq were performed for the following genotypes: wildtype, Xrp1, RpS3, RpS12, RpS12 Xrp1, and RpS3 Xrp1 – giving us FASTQ files. These datasets were generated through experiments in the lab by a previous lab member, Harrison Hector.

## Preprocessing and Quality Control of the Two Datasets

### Bulk RNA Seq

The first step before processing any of our samples is to analyze the quality of the data. To do so, we will use **FastQC (Andrews 2010)**. FastQC looks at different aspects of the sample sequences to determine any irregularities or features that affect your results (adapter contamination, sequence duplication levels, etc.) After running FastQC, we will perform quality control by removing low quality sequences with **Trim Galore (Krueger 2012)**. Trim Galore performs adapter trimming (the default is the first 13 bp of Illumina standard adapters ('AGATCGGAAGAGC'), but it is able to autodetect the adapter sequence). It also removes sequences that become too short during the trimming process. For our dataset, we will use the --paired option, which allows Trim Galore to remove both reads in a pair if at least one of the two sequences becomes shorter than the threshold. After running Trim Galore, we will align reads to the Drosophila genome using **STAR (Dobin et al. 2013)**. After aligning reads to the genome, we can now summarize gene counts using **featureCounts (Liao et al. 2014)**. Lastly, we will use **MultiQC (Ewels et al. 2016)**, which is a tool to create a single report visualizing output from multiple tools across many samples, including the output of FastQC, Trim_Galore, STAR, and featureCounts.

In summary, starting with raw paired end bulk RNA seq FASTQ files, we will run quality control on the raw data, remove low quality sequences, align reads to the Drosophila genome, summarize the gene counts – leaving us with a matrix with the genotypes, their replicates and their respective gene counts, and lastly, run MultiQC to create a single report visualizing output from the tools we have used.

### Ribo Seq

Similarly to Bulk RNA Seq, the first step before processing any of our samples is to analyze the quality of the data using **FastQC (Andrews 2010)**. After which, we will use **Trimmomatic (Bolger et al. 2014)** to trim adaptor sequences from reads. However, unlike the bulk RNA seq preprocessing, we will also implement removal of abundant sequences. This is optional when processing bulk RNA seq, but is necessary when processing Ribo Seq due to the nature of the data collection. In Ribo seq, abundant sequences typically constitute 80% to 90% of the total RNA in a sample, if it is not removed, 90% or more of the resulting sequencing reads will be rRNA contaminants rather than actionable translation data – making it necessary to remove. To do this, we will first prepare a **bowtie2 (Langmead and Salzberg 2012)** index for the known abundant sequences: rRNA, tRNA, and mtRNA. These sequences are considered contaminants of Ribo-seq data, since we want to capture only RPFs. Therefore, reads mapping to these contaminant sequences are removed prior to further analysis. After we remove the abundant sequences, we can then align reads to the genome file using **STAR (Dobin et al. 2013)**. Again, similarly to the preprocessing of bulk RNA seq, we will summarize gene counts using **featureCounts (Liao et al. 2014)**. Ribo seq also has key characteristics that indicate high quality reads, such as a 3 nucleotide periodicity, sharp peak around the translation initiation site (TIS), and sharp decrease near the stop codon – to visualize and test this, we use **Ribotish (Zhang et al. 2017)** on the .bam files we created using STAR. Lastly, as with bulk RNA seq, will use **MultiQC (Ewels et al. 2016)**, to create a single report visualizing output from multiple tools across many samples, including the output of FastQC, Trimmomatic STAR, and featureCounts.

In summary, starting with raw Ribo Seq FASTQ files, we will run quality control on the raw data, remove low quality sequences, remove abundant sequences (rRNA, tRNA, and mtRNA sequences), align reads to the Drosophila genome, perform ribo seq specific quality control, summarize the gene counts – leaving us with a matrix with the genotypes, their replicates and their respective gene counts, and lastly run Multiqc to create a single report visualizing output from the tools we have used.

## Analysis

### Differential Translation of Transcripts

In order to determine differential translation between transcripts, we calculate the translation efficiency (TE). TE is the ratio of the RPFs over mRNA counts within a gene's coding sequence (CDS). TE is essentially the number of ribosomes per gene, normalized to transcript abundance. To determine this, we use **DeSeq2’s (Love et al. 2014)** deltaTE. DeltaTE is also able to categorize the genes into one of two categories: differentially transcribed gene (DTG) and differential translation efficiency gene (DTEG). A gene could be either/both DTG and/or DTEG, and based on the direction of change would fall into one of the eight gene-regulatory possibilities (sig: significant, n.s.: not significant). Translationally forwarded genes are DTGs that have a significant change in mRNA and RPF at the same rate, with no significant change in TE. Conversely, translationally exclusive genes are DTEGs that have a significant change in RPF, with no change in mRNA leading to a significant change in TE. Several genes are both DTGs and DTEGs, and their regulatory class is determined based on a combination of the relative direction of change between transcription and translation efficiency. Specifically, translationally buffered genes have a significant change in TE that counteracts the change in RNA; hence, buffering the effect of transcription. Translationally intensified genes have a significant change in TE that acts with the effect of transcription. In all cases, the change in RNA can be either positive or negative, and where buffering or intensifying takes place, the direction of change is taken into account. For example, a gene that exhibits an increase in transcription and an increase in translation efficiency is classified as intensified, while a gene that exhibits an increase in transcription but a decrease in translational efficiency is classified as buffered.

After the genes have been classified into their respective group, we perform Gene Ontology (GO) Enrichment Analysis to determine the groups of genes that are DTEG and/or DTG. From this classification, we can conclude the impacts that each of the mutations have on gene regulation at the translational level. By distinguishing between changes driven by transcription versus translation efficiency, this framework allows us to identify whether mutations primarily alter mRNA abundance, ribosome loading, or the coordination between the two. GO enrichment of DTGs and DTEGs further reveals the biological processes that are selectively buffered, intensified, or exclusively regulated at the level of translation, providing insight into how each mutation reshapes the translational landscape.

### Differential Analysis of Pausing in Ribosome Profiling Data

We will also follow a protocol published in **STAR protocols (Stein et al. 2022)** to quantify changes in ribosome density distributions along transcripts under different conditions. Changes in translation efficiency can arise from different steps of translation. Ribosome profiling measures not only how many ribosomes are on a transcript, but also where they are positioned along the coding sequence. Transcripts that are limited at initiation tend to show ribosomes distributed evenly across the gene, whereas elongation-limited transcripts show ribosome buildup near the 5′ end due to stalling. By combining TE analysis with ribosome footprint distributions, we can determine whether changes in translation are driven by altered initiation or elongation, providing mechanistic insight beyond overall ribosome occupancy.

## Visualizations

### Quality Control and Preprocessing Metrics

To assess the quality of our sequencing data and preprocessing steps, we will generate MultiQC summary reports that consolidate FastQC quality scores, adapter content, and sequence duplication levels across all samples. Read alignment statistics will display mapping rates, uniquely mapped reads, and multi-mapping percentages for both bulk RNA-seq and Ribo-seq datasets. For Ribo-seq specific quality assessment, we will use Ribotish to generate 3-nucleotide periodicity plots, metagene profiles showing ribosome density around translation initiation sites (TIS) and stop codons, and read length distribution histograms. These visualizations will confirm that our data meets the quality standards necessary for downstream translation analysis.

### Differential Translation Analysis

To visualize differential expression and translation patterns, we will generate MA plots showing log fold change versus mean expression for both RNA-seq and Ribo-seq data, with significantly changed genes highlighted. Volcano plots will display translation efficiency changes with adjusted p-value thresholds to identify the most robust changes. Heatmaps with hierarchical clustering will reveal patterns of differentially translated genes across genotypes. We will create scatterplots comparing mRNA abundance changes on the x-axis versus ribosome occupancy changes on the y-axis, with genes color-coded by their regulatory classification (forwarded, exclusive, buffered, or intensified). This visualization directly illustrates how transcription and translation changes relate to one another. Venn diagrams will also be created to show the overlap of DTGs and DTEGs between different genotypes (RpS3, RpS12, and their Xrp1 double mutants), revealing the shared and unique translational responses of each mutation.

### Gene Ontology and Pathway Analysis

For functional interpretation of our differentially regulated genes, we will generate dot plots or bar charts displaying enriched GO terms for each gene regulatory class, ranked by statistical significance. These plots will reveal which biological processes are preferentially regulated at the transcriptional versus translational level. Network diagrams will illustrate relationships between enriched biological processes, helping to identify coordinated cellular responses to ribosomal stress.

### Ribosome Pausing and Distribution Analysis

To assess changes in ribosome distribution along transcripts, we will create metagene density profiles comparing ribosome distribution patterns between genotypes. Positional heatmaps will show ribosome occupancy along transcript length for select candidate genes, revealing whether ribosomes accumulate at specific positions. Cumulative distribution plots will quantify differences in 5' versus 3' ribosome density ratios, distinguishing initiation-limited from elongation-limited translation.

## Conclusions

This computational pipeline addresses three fundamental questions about translational regulation during ribosomal haploinsufficiency stress. First, by establishing a robust preprocessing workflow, we preprocessed raw sequencing data into high-quality translational profiles suitable for downstream analysis. The preprocessing pipeline incorporates quality control measures specific to each data type, including the critical step of removing abundant rRNA, tRNA, and mtRNA sequences from Ribo-seq data, and validates data quality through Ribo-seq specific metrics.
Second, through deltaTE analysis and gene regulatory classification, we systematically identify which transcripts undergo changes in translation efficiency versus transcription. This framework reveals the distinct mechanisms by which RpS3, RpS12, and Xrp1 reshape the translational landscape, distinguishing between genes that are translationally forwarded, exclusive, buffered, or intensified. GO enrichment of DTGs and DTEGs further reveals the biological processes that are selectively regulated at each level, providing insight into how each mutation affects specific cellular pathways.
Third, by analyzing ribosome density distributions along transcripts, we distinguish whether translation changes result from altered initiation or elongation. This positional analysis provides mechanistic insight into how ribosomal protein mutations affect specific steps of protein synthesis, moving beyond overall ribosome occupancy to understand the precise nature of translational defects.
In summary, these analyses will define the molecular features and biological pathways most sensitive to ribosomal stress, ultimately advancing our overall goal of understanding translational landscape of ribosomal stress and identify molecular and pathway-level features that determine how RpS12 and Xrp1 mediate translation changes under ribosomal stress.
